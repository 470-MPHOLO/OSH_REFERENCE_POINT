Question 1: Introduction to wireless networks, operating systems and hardware 8 marks

What is required: introduce wireless networks, network communication, operating systems and computer hardware; explain why the university needs both wired and wireless for about 800 mobile users across seven buildings; discuss how selected hardware and operating systems support the computing and networking requirements.

Model answer skeleton

SubsectionContent to coverScenario anchor to include
1.1 Wireless networkingWLAN defined by IEEE 802.11; radio bands 2.4, 5 and 6 GHz; CSMA/CA rather than CSMA/CD; channels and co-channel interference; standards from 802.11n to 802.11ax (Wi-Fi 6); the role of access points, SSIDs, roaming and authentication (WPA3, 802.1X)Staff move between seven buildings, so the design must support roaming without dropping sessions; dense urban spectrum means 5 GHz and 6 GHz planning, not 2.4 GHz alone
1.1 Network communicationOSI and TCP/IP models; encapsulation; switching at layer 2 and routing at layer 3; the difference between a broadcast domain and a collision domain; latency, throughput and jitter as the three numbers that matterSeven buildings require layer 3 separation (VLANs and subnets) while still appearing as one campus to the user
1.2 Operating systemsKernel, drivers, process and memory management, file systems, user and permission model; the difference between a desktop OS and a server OS; why driver support decides whether a workstation build succeedsThe 15 StartUp Hub workstations arrive with no OS and no drivers, so OS selection and driver availability are a procurement decision, not an afterthought
1.3 HardwareCPU cores and threads; RAM capacity and speed; storage type and interface (SATA, NVMe); NIC speed; power supply sizing; thermal design; form factor800 staff need portable devices; the 15 workstations are fixed build units; three printers need network interfaces and memory
1.4 Why hybridWireless gives mobility and lower cabling cost per seat; wired gives deterministic bandwidth, PoE, lower latency and building-to-building backhaul. Neither alone meets the requirementQuantify: 800 users, assume 1.5 to 2 devices each, so roughly 1200 to 1600 concurrent devices, which no single wireless-only design carries reliably across seven buildings
The paragraph that earns the top band. Do not simply say "wireless is convenient and wired is fast". Say: the seven buildings are joined by wired fibre backhaul because no wireless bridge can carry aggregate inter-building traffic reliably in a dense urban spectrum; inside each building, Wi-Fi 6 access points carry the mobile devices; and wired access ports are retained for fixed devices that need PoE or deterministic bandwidth (printers, APs, desk phones, the 15 workstations). That three-layer argument (backhaul, wireless access, wired access) is what separates an 8 from a 5.

How to write the justification so it scores. Compatibility

State three compatibility chains explicitly. First, Windows 11 Pro requires TPM 2.0 and UEFI Secure Boot, so the laptop BOM must include TPM, which in turn affects cost. Second, WPA3-Enterprise requires an OS supplicant that supports it; Windows 11 and Ubuntu 24.04 both do, older Android devices may not, which is why a transitional WPA2-Enterprise SSID may be needed. Third, the three printers must support IPP over TLS and 802.1X, or they must be placed on the PRINT VLAN with MAC authentication bypass rather than full 802.1X. That last point is the kind of practical compatibility detail that lifts the answer.

The trap in this question. Students write "the network will be reliable because we use good equipment". That is not an evaluation. Every claim needs a number and a measurement method. If you can also show one real measurement from your lab build (a ping showing 0.4 ms to the gateway, an iperf3 showing 940 Mbps on a wired port), the answer moves into the top band because it is evidenced, not asserted.

What is required: an integrated design across seven buildings, showing wired and wireless infrastructure, main network devices, workstations, servers and services, access points and inter-building connections; a clearly labelled diagram; an explanation of how the design supports 800 mobile users while meeting performance, reliability, availability, compatibility and security.

Design principles to state first

  1. Three-tier topology. A collapsed core in the main building, a distribution layer per building, and an access layer per floor. The core carries inter-building and inter-VLAN traffic; the distribution aggregates the building; the access connects devices.
  2. Fibre ring between buildings. Single-mode fibre, 12-core minimum, in a ring rather than a star, so one cut does not isolate a building. RSTP or MSTP prevents loops; OSPF or a routed core handles failover.
  3. Wireless as the primary access method for staff. Wi-Fi 6 APs on 5 GHz and 6 GHz, PoE+ from the access switch, WPA3-Enterprise with RADIUS, 802.11k, v and r for fast roaming, one SSID for staff and one isolated SSID for guests.
  4. Wired as the deterministic path. Printers, APs, the 15 workstations and any fixed device on Cat 6A, with 802.1X or MAC authentication bypass.
  5. Security by segmentation. The VLAN plan from Q3, enforced by firewall rules between VLANs, not by hope.
  6. Services centralised and redundant. Two domain controllers, DHCP failover, file and print server, RADIUS, backup NAS off the core switch, monitoring.

Question 7: Secure environment design and user training 10 marks

What is required: a security design guide for the university and StartUp Hub; explain how devices, operating systems, wireless, user accounts and data are protected; implement at least three controls with labelled screenshots; evaluate each control's effectiveness, purpose and contribution; include user-training measures.

Part 1: Threat model (short, but do it)

Name the threats before naming the controls. In this environment the realistic threats are: a stolen staff laptop with unencrypted data; a rogue device joining the staff wireless; credential theft through phishing; lateral movement from an infected workstation to the file server; an unpatched printer used as a pivot; and a shared local administrator password reused across 815 devices. Every control below answers one of those.

Question 8: Password policy design and justification 10 marks

What is required: a password policy covering normal user accounts, application and service accounts, IT administrator accounts, and administrator or root accounts; justify each rule; evaluate impact on security and usability for about 800 users; compare with a weak policy and explain how the proposed rules improve protection against common threats while remaining practical.

The four account classes

ClassRuleJustificationUsability impact
Normal user (about 800)Minimum 12 characters, no forced periodic change, no composition rules beyond a blocked list, MFA required, lockout after 5 failures for 15 minutes, screen lock after 10 minutesLength resists brute force and offline cracking far more than complexity; forced rotation drives predictable patterns (Summer2026!) and incremental changes; a blocked list stops the passwords that appear in every breach corpus; MFA means a stolen password alone is not enoughFewer forced changes means fewer helpdesk calls; a passphrase of three or four words is easy to remember; MFA adds one prompt per session, mitigated by trusted device and remember-me for a defined period
Application and service accountsMinimum 32 random characters, never interactive logon, unique per service, stored in a secrets vault or managed service account, rotated on a schedule and on staff change, least privilege, no domain-wide rightsNo human remembers these, so length and randomness are free; interactive logon is the attack path, so removing it removes the path; unique per service stops one compromised credential being reused across systems; least privilege limits the blast radiusNo usability cost to end users; some operational effort to rotate, which the vault automates
IT administrator accountsSeparate admin account from the daily-use account, minimum 16 characters, MFA on every elevation, admin sessions from a hardened management host, just-in-time elevation with a time limit, no admin rights on the daily accountSeparating the admin identity means a phishing attack on the user's mailbox does not yield domain rights; MFA on elevation blocks the pass-the-hash and stolen-ticket paths; JIT reduces the window in which a compromised admin session is usefulTwo accounts to manage and an extra authentication step, which is the point; the cost is deliberate friction on privileged actions
Administrator and root (local and domain)Unique password per device, minimum 20 characters, vaulted and rotated automatically, break-glass account sealed and logged, root logon restricted to the console or a management network, all use auditedA shared local admin password is the single most exploited weakness in a Windows estate; unique per device stops lateral movement; the sealed break-glass account exists so that a real emergency does not tempt staff to weaken controls under pressureAdministrators cannot memorise every device password, so a vault is required, which is an operational change, not a user-facing one

Comparison with a weak policy

AspectWeak policyProposed policyThreat reduced and why
Length8 characters12 for users, 16 to 32 for privileged and serviceBrute force and offline cracking: each added character multiplies the search space, so length is the dominant factor
RotationForced every 60 or 90 daysNo forced rotation; change on evidence of compromise onlyPredictable patterns: forced rotation produces Password1, Password2, Summer2026 and similar, which are in every cracking wordlist. Removing rotation removes the predictable pattern.
ComplexityMust contain upper, lower, digit and symbolNo composition rules; a blocked list of common and breached passwords insteadComposition rules produce P@ssw0rd, which passes the rule and falls in seconds; a blocked list stops the actual bad passwords
LockoutNone, or 3 attempts with a permanent lock5 attempts, 15-minute lockout, counter reset after 15 minutesBrute force is slowed without creating a denial-of-service where an attacker locks out a known user permanently
MFANoneRequired for users, administrators and elevationCredential stuffing and phishing: a stolen password alone is no longer sufficient
Privileged accountsSame account for daily use and administrationSeparate accounts, MFA, JIT elevation, unique local admin passwordsLateral movement and pass-the-hash: compromising a mailbox no longer yields domain control
Service accountsSame as user accounts, shared by the team32 random characters, vaulted, non-interactive, rotatedKerberoasting and credential reuse: a service account compromise no longer unlocks other systems
The usability evaluation the question asks for. Weigh both sides. The proposed policy removes forced rotation, which reduces helpdesk load and user frustration, and adds MFA and a vault, which increases the first-week effort and the support burden during rollout. Say so. Then state the mitigation: phased rollout by building, a password manager provided to all staff, self-service reset, and a communications plan. An answer that only praises the policy is not an evaluation. The examiner wants the trade-off named, quantified where possible, and mitigated.
Where to put the policy in practice. Mention the technical enforcement: Group Policy for Windows, PAM and pwquality for Ubuntu, a password filter or Azure AD Password Protection for the blocked list, and the vault for service and local admin accounts. Naming the enforcement mechanism shows the policy is implementable, not aspirational.
